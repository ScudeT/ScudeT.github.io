I'm a PhD student in Mechanical Engineering at Politecnico di Milano, where I work on
control algorithms for bio-inspired robotics. In practice that means I get to move across
the whole development pipeline — from soft and bio-inspired hardware, through simulation,
to the machine learning that ties it all together.

## What I work on

My work lives at the meeting point of bio-inspired and underwater robotics: I'm fascinated by
how borrowing from the way animals move can make robots that swim, glide, and steer through
water genuinely capable. Right now that means control for batoid-inspired underwater
locomotion and soft, bio-inspired systems — problems that force me to touch every layer, from
the circuit board to the learning loop.

Looking ahead, I'm increasingly drawn to human-robot interaction, and in particular to the
idea of teaching robots more natural, legible motion through animation — so that moving
alongside people feels intuitive rather than mechanical.

And underneath all of it is the reason I started down this path: the hope that robotics can be
part of how we take on climate-related problems. That's the long horizon I'd like my work to
bend toward.

## How I work

I learn by doing, and often by failing. Most of my projects started as a reach — something I
felt unprepared for — and I figured it out on the way. I'd rather build the thing and find
out where it breaks than spend too long with the theory first, though the design mentality
my education gave me is something I lean on constantly.

I also overthink every way a thing can go wrong. In mechatronics that turns out to be less a
bug than a feature: half the job is imagining the failure modes before the hardware finds
them for you. It has never stopped me from trying — it just means I try with my eyes open.
