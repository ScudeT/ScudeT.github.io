> February 2022 - May 2023

## Why buy a new boat when you can rescue an old one?

For most of my life I lived in front of a lake I couldn't reach on my own. I sailed, rowed, and kitesurfed on Lake Garda for years; always with equipment that belonged to someone else. I wanted my own, but I never quite managed to afford it: whatever I saved, a stubborn passion for travel would pull straight back out of my savings.

Then the chance arrived in the shape of a friend's abandoned boat; an old English sailing boat whose long life had come to a stop, left out under the sun in the middle of the Po Valley. Together with a few others in the same situation, we decided to take it on and bring it back to life.

We hauled it from the field where it had been left to rot all the way to Milan, so we could work on it in whatever time we had; afternoons, weekends, any day we were all free to pour into what looked, honestly, like a lost cause. The rule was simple: spend as little as possible. Professional repairs would have cost a fortune, so almost everything had to be done with our own hands.

And the boat was in a sorry state. The coating was a web of cracks, the interior was rotten, and the inner lining was carpeted with dead insects. But we were already too far in to turn back. We bought the tools we needed, asked professionals for advice, and got to work.

For the coating, we were told to scrape each crack back until it vanished, then seal it with a compound we could apply in our makeshift boatyard. So we scraped. And scraped. And scraped; and the cracks simply would not end. They ran all the way down to the fibreglass hull, until there was nothing left to do but strip the coating off entirely and start again. That was the moment we could have loaded it back onto the trailer and returned it exactly as we'd found it. We didn't. We couldn't. We re-coated almost all of it ourselves; the one part we handed to a professional was the final coat on the lower hull; the job that genuinely had to be done in a proper boatyard to be done right.

The interior lining came out completely; the glue holding it had long since failed, and the bugs had worked their way into everything. The rotten wood was torn out and thrown away, later replaced with marine plywood. And that was only the headline work; dozens of smaller jobs stood between us and the water. It was a long haul, and right up until the day we lowered her into the lake, we genuinely didn't know whether she would float.

She did. And not only that; in the years since, she has ridden out serious storms without letting in a single drop. It remains one of the most satisfying things I've ever done, and it finally let me live the lake that had sat in front of me my whole life.

It was also the first big project I ever took on, and it shaped how I've approached every one since; the nerve to start something far beyond my experience, the stubbornness to keep going when walking away would have been the easy choice, and the judgement to know which parts to hand to someone who could do them better.

## The boat's life before us

Part of what kept us going was knowing where the boat had come from.

She was first owned by an Englishman who used to sail her across the Channel to visit his French fiancée. Every summer he came to Lake Garda for a holiday at the same hotel, and there he met Stefano Angeli; now our neighbour; and the two spent years of summers adventuring on the lake together. Eventually the Englishman decided to bring the boat to Garda for good: he crossed the Channel, boarded her in France, and drove her over the Alps to sail with his summer friend. When the holiday ended, rather than face the long journey home, he gave the boat to Stefano.

She became a core memory of Stefano's children, who grew up sailing her. Those children now have children of their own; newer boats were bought, and Spritze; for that is her name; was left in a parking lot, then handed to a friend in the Po Valley who promised to restore her. He never did. And that is where we came in.
